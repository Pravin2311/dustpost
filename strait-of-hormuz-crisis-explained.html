📍 Geography: A Narrow Passage With Global Consequences

The Strait of Hormuz lies between Iran to the north and the Omani exclave of Musandam to the south. At its narrowest point, it spans just 21 nautical miles (39 km). Within this corridor:

  • Two 2-mile-wide traffic separation lanes exist—one inbound, one outbound—established under international maritime rules.
  • The remaining space is considered high seas, open to all nations.
  • It connects the Persian Gulf (home to 40% of the world’s proven oil reserves) to the Arabian Sea and global markets.

🛢️ Energy Lifeline: By the Numbers

According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) and International Energy Agency (IEA):

  • 17–20 million barrels of oil per day flow through the strait—roughly 20% of global consumption.
  • 30% of all seaborne-traded crude oil passes through here.
  • Major exporters: Saudi Arabia (7.5M bpd), Iraq (4.5M), UAE (3.5M), Kuwait (2.8M), Qatar (1.5M LNG).
  • Major importers: China (55%), India (15%), Japan (10%), South Korea (8%).

💡 Critical Insight: There is no scalable alternative. Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline (Petroline) can move only ~5 million barrels/day—and it’s already operating near capacity. Iraq, UAE, Qatar, and Kuwait have zero pipeline exits to non-Gulf ports.

⚖️ Legal Status: Can Iran Close It?

Despite popular belief, **Iran cannot legally close the strait—even in wartime**.

Under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), the Strait of Hormuz is classified as an “international strait” where all vessels—military and civilian—enjoy the right of “transit passage” at all times.

While Iran is not a signatory to UNCLOS, the principle of transit passage is considered customary international law—binding on all states.

That said, Iran can—and does—disrupt traffic using asymmetric tactics:

  • Naval mines (as in 1988)
  • Fast attack boats swarming tankers
  • Drones and anti-ship missiles (e.g., 2019 attacks on Saudi Aramco)
  • Coastal radar jamming and false GPS signals

Full closure? Unlikely. But even minor delays cause oil prices to spike—and that’s Iran’s real leverage.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can Iran legally close the Strait of Hormuz?

No. Under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), the strait is an international strait where all vessels enjoy the right of “transit passage”—even during wartime. Iran is not a signatory to UNCLOS but is still bound by customary international law.

Why can’t Gulf oil exporters bypass the Strait of Hormuz?

Because there are no pipelines large enough. Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline moves only ~5 million barrels/day—far less than its 12+ million bpd output. Iraq, UAE, Qatar, and Kuwait have no alternatives.

What happens if oil stops flowing for 30 days?

Global GDP would contract by 2–3%. Strategic petroleum reserves (U.S.: 360M barrels, IEA: 1.5B total) would last ~90 days—but panic buying would accelerate shortages. Alternative routes (Red Sea, Oman) lack capacity.
